\begin{abstract}
In imbalanced classification the class of interest is rare and is often the extreme tail of the data.
This project treats the minority class as a tail phenomenon, describes its severity with a Generalized
Pareto Distribution (GPD) fitted by peaks-over-threshold, and uses this extreme value model to decide
which synthetic minority examples a generative model should add to the training data. The case study is
next-day extreme heat on a \ang{0.1} ERA5-Land grid over Himachal Pradesh: 529 cells, 1981--2025, about
1\% positives, season-wise extremes defined from 1981--2010 thresholds, and a test decade (2016--2025)
with twice as many extremes as the training period. The classifier is LightGBM, using the last days and
weeks of weather.

\textbf{Part I} develops the method: after GAN attempts that produced invalid or uncontrollable extremes,
an EVT-guided conditional diffusion model with physics built in raised PR-AUC on the most severe 10\% of
test extremes from 0.411 to 0.549 (better in 5 of 5 training seeds), but only when EVT was used to
\emph{emphasise the upper tail}; drawing severities from the GPD as fitted gave no gain.
\textbf{Part II} fixes the classifier and compares ten ways of generating extremes (SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE,
ADASYN, Gaussian copula, TVAE, CTGAN, CopulaGAN, a conditional WGAN-GP and diffusion), each with and
without EVT tail focus, over five seeds. EVT tail focus improved every generator, most clearly on
heatwave onsets (better in 4--5 of 5 seeds for all six generators). The best methods, CTGAN and TVAE with
EVT tail focus, reached severe PR-AUC 0.601 (real data only: 0.441) and beat a strong persistence
baseline on overall skill (0.611 vs 0.586, better in 99\% of month-block resamples). The most realistic
synthetic data (diffusion) was not the most useful. \textbf{Part III} shows that the EVT tail emphasis works
even without a generator: copies of real extremes chosen with the EVT tail target beat random copies on severe
days in 5 of 5 seeds (+0.057) and gave the highest overall PR-AUC of the study (0.613), while generators with
EVT tail focus added a further +0.06 on the most severe days (5 of 5 seeds) at the price of onset skill
($-0.02$, 5 of 5 seeds).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Motivation}
Extreme heat is one of the clearest signals of a warming climate, and in a mountain state such as
Himachal Pradesh its effects reach agriculture, glaciers, water supply and health. Predicting
\emph{tomorrow's} extreme heat at a given place is a classification problem in which the positive class is
rare (about one day in a hundred) and in which the most important cases---the most severe days---are the
rarest of all. Standard remedies re-weight the loss or create synthetic minority samples by interpolation
(SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote}). Deep generative models such as GANs~\cite{goodfellow2014gan,xu2019ctgan},
variational autoencoders and diffusion models~\cite{ho2020ddpm,kotelnikov2023tabddpm} can produce richer
synthetic data, but they are trained to match the bulk of the distribution and tend to under-represent its
tails. Extreme value theory (EVT)~\cite{coles2001} offers an asymptotically justified model for exactly
that region.

\subsection{Problem statement and objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Treat the minority class as a tail phenomenon.
  \item Use EVT to characterise and constrain the tail.
  \item Use generative models to augment and rebalance the training data.
  \item Ensure that synthetic data are statistically consistent with real extremes, and test whether they
        improve a classifier.
\end{enumerate}
Following discussion with the supervisor, the final research question is: \emph{with one fixed classifier
(LightGBM) predicting next-day extreme heat from the previous days and weeks, which way of generating extra
extreme days helps most, and what does EVT add?}

\subsection{Related work}
Elor and Averbuch-Elor~\cite{elor2022smote} found that balancing rarely helps strong classifiers such as
gradient-boosted trees. D'souza et al.~\cite{dsouza2025overlap} report that tabular diffusion produces
better minority samples than GANs. On the EVT side, ExGAN~\cite{bhatia2021exgan} generates extreme samples
of a chosen severity, evtGAN~\cite{boulaguiem2022evtgan} combines GANs with extreme-value margins for
spatial weather extremes, and heavy-tailed diffusion models~\cite{pandey2025heavy} change the noise
distribution to reach far tails. Jalalzai et al.~\cite{jalalzai2018extreme} study classification in
extreme regions of the input space. For extreme-heat forecasting, neural weather models are known to
under-predict the most extreme events~\cite{lopezgomez2023heat}. Few works test whether EVT-consistent
synthetic extremes improve a downstream classifier, or compare generator families under a fixed
classifier for a geophysical tail problem; this report does both.

ERA5-Land is the only source that is complete for 45 years at a resolution fine enough for mountainous

\subsection{Pre-processing and verification}
All inputs are on ERA5-Land's \ang{0.1} grid over \ang{30.3}--\ang{33.3}N, \ang{75.5}--\ang{79.1}E.
Accumulated variables (rain, radiation) are shifted back by the one day by which ERA5-Land stamps them
late; SRTM is averaged into cell elevation and slope; cells outside the state are masked, leaving 529 cells
and 16{,}436 days. The processed file was verified against the raw ERA5-Land files for five cells
(522--\SI{5279}{m}) and six years: all variables match after unit conversion, with no day where
$T_{\min}>T_{\max}$. Mean temperature and humidity were missing for 1981--1985 and were refilled from the
raw files. A bias correction against MODIS from an earlier pipeline was not used: its validation against
IMD showed a $-15$\,\textdegree C bias that is most plausibly the IMD missing-value code (99.9) treated as
data.

\section{Problem formulation}
\subsection{Extreme labels}
For cell $c$ and day $t$ let $T^{\max}_{c,t}$ be the daily maximum temperature. A threshold $u_{c,g}$ is
the 99th percentile of $T^{\max}$ over the days of group $g$ in the base period 1981--2010, and day $t$ is
a heat extreme if $T^{\max}_{c,t}>u_{c,g(t)}$. The main grouping is the IMD season: winter (Dec--Feb),
pre-monsoon (Mar--May), monsoon (Jun--Sep), post-monsoon (Oct--Nov); each season is compared only with the
same season, so a very warm winter day counts even though summer is hotter. Using only 1981--2010 for
thresholds keeps the validation and test years unseen. Thresholds fall by about 6--7\,\textdegree C per km
of altitude (Figure~\ref{fig:thr}), consistent with the atmospheric lapse rate. The project focuses on
heat: in Himachal Pradesh extreme heat is the growing hazard, and cold tails were found to mix regimes.

\centering
\optfig{figures/fig_thresholds.png}{0.78\linewidth}
\caption{Mean heat threshold (99th percentile of Tmax, 1981--2010) by elevation band and season.}

\subsection{Prediction task, features and splits}
Each sample is one cell on day $t$; the target is whether day $t+1$ is a seasonal heat extreme. The final
feature set (dataset v3) has 57 features describing the previous days and weeks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item terrain: latitude, longitude, elevation, slope;
  \item weather on days $t$, $t-1$, $t-2$: Tmax, Tmin, Tmean, dewpoint, humidity, pressure, solar radiation,
        soil moisture, rain; today's Tmax and Tmin anomalies;
  \item longer history: mean Tmax anomaly over 7, 14 and 30 days, Tmin anomaly over 7 and 30 days, length
        of the current warm spell, days since the last extreme, extremes in the last 30 days, rain over 7 and
        30 days, soil moisture over 14 and 30 days and its 14-day change, 7-day dewpoint and radiation, 3-day
        pressure change;
  \item distance of today, yesterday and the day before to tomorrow's threshold, in \textdegree C and in units
        of the cell's variability $s_{c,g}$, plus their trend and the day-to-day Tmax change.
\end{itemize}
Calendar features are excluded: in a first version the model relied on day of year (39\% of split gain),
which does not transfer to a warmer test decade. The data are split by year, because neighbouring days and
cells are strongly correlated (one heatwave makes hundreds of cells extreme at once). Training uses a random
half of the 1981--2010 cell-days to fit free compute (Table~\ref{tab:splits}).

\subsection{What the labels show}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Warming.} Heat extremes nearly double in the test decade (1.94\% vs 1.02\%) while cold
        extremes fall; 2024 has the most widespread heat.
  \item \textbf{Strongest signal in October--November.} Post-monsoon heat extremes reach 11.0\% of days in
        2020 and 6--7\% in 2017, 2021 and 2024, against a 1\% baseline.
  \item \textbf{Clustering.} On 6 October 1983, 522 of 529 cells were extreme together; the training
        positives come from a few hundred events.
  \item \textbf{Season-edge effect.} Seasonal extremes gather at the warm edge of a season (pre-monsoon
        heat in May, post-monsoon heat in early October).
\end{itemize}

\section{Extreme value model of the minority class}\label{sec:evt}
\subsection{Peaks over threshold}
By the Pickands--Balkema--de Haan theorem~\cite{pickands1975,balkema1974}, for a high threshold $u$ the
excess $Y=X-u$ given $X>u$ is approximately Generalized Pareto distributed:
\begin{equation}
  F_{\xi,\sigma}(y)=1-\left(1+\frac{\xi y}{\sigma}\right)^{-1/\xi},\qquad y>0,\; 1+\xi y/\sigma>0 .
\end{equation}
The shape $\xi$ determines the tail: $\xi<0$ gives a bounded tail with upper end point $\sigma/|\xi|$
(expected for temperature), $\xi=0$ an exponential tail and $\xi>0$ a heavy tail. Parameters are fitted by
maximum likelihood on 1981--2010 exceedances, with confidence intervals from a block bootstrap that
resamples whole days (exceedances are spatially clustered) and threshold-stability checks.

\subsection{Standardised severity}
Pooling raw excesses from all 529 cells mixes low cells with small day-to-day variability and high cells
with large variability; the mixture looks heavy-tailed even when each cell's tail is bounded. We therefore
standardise each excess by the cell's own variability $s_{c,g}$ (the standard deviation of its daily
temperature anomaly in that season, 1981--2010):
\begin{equation}
  z_{c,t}=\frac{T^{\max}_{c,t}-u_{c,g(t)}}{s_{c,g(t)}} .
\end{equation}
This severity $z$ is what the EVT model describes and what the generators are guided by. It is never a
classifier feature, since it encodes the next day's outcome.

\subsection{EVT tail focus}\label{sec:tailfocus}
The fitted GPD gives every extreme a \emph{tail position} $q=F_{\xi,\sigma}(z)\in[0,1)$: $q=0.9$ means only
10\% of extremes in that season are more severe. Real extremes are spread evenly over $q$. \emph{EVT tail
focus} asks for synthetic extremes with half of their tail positions drawn from the whole GPD and half from
its upper 20\% ($q\in[0.8,1)$), so that about 60\% of the synthetic extremes lie in the region that holds
only 20\% of the real ones. Severities are capped at the bounded tail's end point and at 1.5 times the
largest observed severity of the season, so EVT can extrapolate moderately but not without limit. This is
the main way EVT enters the project.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All classifiers are trained on 1981--2010. Every choice (early stopping, decision threshold, amount of
synthetic data, blend weight, final model) is made on the validation years 2011--2015; the test decade
2016--2025 is used only for scoring. Scores:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PR-AUC} (average precision) on all test days; a random classifier scores 0.019.
  \item \textbf{Onset PR-AUC}: only days where today is not yet above tomorrow's threshold, i.e.\ heatwave
        starts, which persistence cannot anticipate.
  \item \textbf{Severe PR-AUC}: normal days against the 10\% most severe test extremes ($z\ge0.77$,
        3{,}745 cell-days); this is the region the EVT guidance targets.
  \item Precision, recall and the critical success index
        $\mathrm{CSI}=\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN})$ at the F1-optimal validation threshold.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Uncertainty.} Because extremes come in spatially and temporally clustered events, 95\% intervals
resample whole test months (200 resamples). Two models are compared on the same resamples; the share of
resamples in which one is better is reported, with $\ge0.95$ read as a convincing win and $\le0.05$ as a
convincing loss. From Step~9 on, every setting is also trained with five seeds, which change LightGBM's
randomness and which synthetic rows are drawn, because the bootstrap does not capture training randomness.
\emph{Seed-averaged} models average the predicted probabilities of the five seeds.

\textbf{Baseline to beat.} \emph{Persistence} uses no model: its score is today's distance to tomorrow's
threshold, $T^{\max}_{c,t}-u_{c,g(t+1)}$. Because heat extremes persist, this simple rule is strong.

\textbf{Classifier.} LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} with learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, up to 1{,}000 trees
with early stopping on validation PR-AUC, and a monotone constraint that a larger distance to tomorrow's
threshold can only raise the risk. With the distance features, the standardised distance carries 78\% of
the split gain.

\section{First generators (Steps 3--7)}
\subsection{Classic rebalancing and CTGAN}
Class weights and SMOTE gave no reliable improvement over real data alone (Table~\ref{tab:step8}), in line
with~\cite{elor2022smote}. A CTGAN~\cite{xu2019ctgan} trained on the real extremes produced rows of which
only 15.9\% were physically possible (for example $T^{\min}>T^{\max}$). Resampling its output so that
severities follow the GPD made the severity distribution match (Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance from
0.16--0.24 to about 0.02), but the GAN rarely generated the far tail, so EVT could only re-weight what was
there. Conditioning CTGAN on season $\times$ GPD severity bin also failed: 70\% of generated rows had a
severity outside the requested bin. This motivated a generator in which the severity is a direct,
continuous input.

\subsection{EVT-guided conditional tabular diffusion}\label{sec:diff}
The generator is a denoising diffusion model~\cite{ho2020ddpm} trained on the 29{,}604 real training
extremes. It models the 49 base features after a quantile transform to a standard normal per feature. The
forward process adds Gaussian noise, $q(x_\tau\mid x_0)=\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_\tau}\,x_0,
(1-\bar\alpha_\tau)I)$, with a cosine schedule over $T=1000$ steps~\cite{nichol2021improved}. The denoiser
$\epsilon_\theta(x_\tau,\tau,c)$ is a residual MLP (width 512, four blocks) with the condition
$c=[\text{one-hot season},\, z/0.5,\, 1]$, set to zero for 10\% of training rows. It is trained with the
noise-prediction loss $\lVert\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_\tau,\tau,c)\rVert^2$ (15{,}000 steps, batch 1{,}024,
AdamW, EMA, about 30 minutes on a free T4 GPU) and sampled with 50 DDIM steps~\cite{song2021ddim} and
classifier-free guidance~\cite{ho2022cfg} of weight $w=2$:
$\hat\epsilon=(1+w)\,\epsilon_\theta(x,\tau,c)-w\,\epsilon_\theta(x,\tau,\varnothing)$.

\textbf{Physics by construction.} Each generated row is snapped to the nearest real grid cell (which fixes
its terrain, threshold and variability), the three temperatures of each day are sorted so that
$T^{\min}\le T^{\text{mean}}\le T^{\max}$, other variables are clipped to their physical ranges, and the
eight distance-to-threshold features are recomputed exactly. The same clean-up is applied to every
generator in Part II.

\textbf{Where EVT enters.} Because $z$ is an input, the severities can be chosen freely. Three synthetic sets
come from the same network, so any difference is due to EVT alone: \texttt{ddpm\_emp} (severities copied from
real extremes, no EVT), \texttt{ddpm\_evt} (drawn from the fitted GPD) and \texttt{ddpm\_evt\_tail} (EVT tail
focus, Section~\ref{sec:tailfocus}).

\textbf{Does the model obey the requested severity?} A separate LightGBM ``judge'' trained on real extremes
predicts $z$ from a row's features. The Spearman correlation between requested and judged severity is 0.776
on held-out real extremes, 0.62--0.67 for the diffusion sets and 0.04 for CTGAN: the diffusion model
produces weather that matches the requested severity.

\subsection{How much synthetic data (Step 9)}
Small amounts (0.25, 0.5 synthetic per real extreme) change little; at the full 8.7 every set loses onset
skill. One synthetic per real extreme was best on validation (Figure~\ref{fig:ratio}) and is used from here on.

\subsection{Applying EVT tail focus to any generator}
Each generator is used twice: \emph{plain} (as it is) and with \emph{EVT tail focus}
(Section~\ref{sec:tailfocus}). For the conditional generators (WGAN-GP, diffusion) the severities are requested
directly from the GPD. Unconditional generators cannot be told a severity, so a generator-independent route is
used:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item generate a pool ten times larger than needed (296{,}040 rows) and apply the physics clean-up;
  \item score every row with the severity judge (Spearman 0.769 on unseen real extremes) and convert the
        judged severity into a tail position $q$ by its rank among real extremes of the same season;
  \item select rows so that $q$ follows the tail-focus target (20 bins; 60\% of rows with $q\ge0.8$) and
        seasons keep their real shares.
\end{enumerate}
If a generator makes too few severe rows, its severe rows must be repeated; the share of repeated rows is
reported, so this route also measures whether a generator can reach the far tail at all.

\subsection{Generator settings}
All generators are trained on the same 29{,}604 real extremes with the same 49 base features plus season.
TVAE, CTGAN and CopulaGAN use the library defaults of 300 epochs and batch 500 (SDV 2.2~\cite{patki2016sdv}).
The conditional WGAN-GP uses three-layer MLPs of width 512 for generator and critic, gradient penalty 10,
five critic updates per generator update and 6{,}000 generator updates. The interpolation methods work
directly on the 57 classifier features; Borderline-SMOTE and ADASYN use a random 300{,}000 normal days as
neighbours.

\section{Control experiment: EVT tail emphasis without a generator (Step 13)}
\subsection{Motivation and design}
Part II shows that unrealistic synthetic days help as much as realistic ones, provided they are chosen with
the EVT tail focus. This raises the question whether the gain needs a generator at all. Step 13 keeps
everything as in Step 12 (same LightGBM, five seeds, one extra extreme per real extreme) and adds three methods
that use only real data:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Random oversampling}: copies of random real extremes.
  \item \textbf{EVT-tail oversampling}: copies of real extremes chosen with the same EVT tail target as the
        generators (60\% from the top 20\% of each season's tail); the rare severe days are copied about three
        times each.
  \item \textbf{EVT-tail weighting}: no copies; each real extreme receives a training weight that adds the same
        tail emphasis (from about $1.5\times$ for mild extremes to about $4\times$ for the most severe), with a
        total extra weight equal to one copy of every extreme.
\end{itemize}
They are compared with real data only, the four best EVT-tail generators of Step 12 and persistence.

\subsection{What the control shows}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{EVT works without a generator.} Copying real extremes chosen with the EVT tail target beats copying
        random real extremes on severe days in every seed (+0.057) and in every month resample of the seed-averaged
        models. The EVT tail emphasis is therefore an active ingredient on its own.
  \item \textbf{The cheapest method is the best all-rounder.} EVT-tail oversampling of real extremes gives the highest
        overall PR-AUC of the whole study (0.608 per seed, 0.613 seed-averaged), better onset skill than real data
        only, and the second-smallest seed-to-seed spread, with no generator to train.
  \item \textbf{Generators add the far tail.} CTGAN and TVAE with EVT tail focus are better than EVT-tail copies on the
        most severe days in all five seeds (about +0.06). Copies can only repeat the most severe real days, whereas
        generators place new extremes beyond them; only the generator-based models beat persistence on severe days.
  \item \textbf{The price is onset skill.} The same generators are worse on heatwave onsets in all five seeds (about
        $-0.02$; a convincing loss for the seed-averaged models). Synthetic extremes pull the classifier towards the
        ``already very hot'' region and away from the start of new events.
  \item \textbf{Copies beat weights.} EVT-tail weighting adds the same total emphasis as EVT-tail copies but helps less
        on severe days (0.467 vs 0.521). A likely reason is that LightGBM's minimum leaf size counts rows, not
        weights: duplicated severe days can form their own leaves, single weighted days cannot.
\end{enumerate}
On the pre-specified validation score the generator-based models still rank first (CTGAN + EVT tail 0.600 against
0.567 for EVT-tail copies), because the validation score includes severe skill.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{EVT is most useful for deciding \emph{where} to add data.} The fitted GPD describes observed
severities well, so drawing from it reproduces almost the same severities as the data and adds little (Part I).
The useful role of EVT is to locate the sparse upper tail and to bound how far it extends: asking for 60\% of
synthetic extremes from the upper 20\% of the GPD fills exactly the region where the classifier has too few
examples. This held for every generator family in Part II.

\paragraph{What a generator adds beyond EVT.} Part III separates two effects. The EVT tail emphasis itself---putting
more weight on the rare severe days---improves the classifier even with copies of real days, and is the cheapest
way to gain overall and onset skill. A generator adds something copies cannot: new extremes beyond the most severe
observed days, which is what lifts severe-day skill above persistence. Which to use depends on the goal: a warning
system that must not miss the most severe heat favours generator + EVT tail focus; an all-round forecast favours
EVT-tail oversampling of real extremes.

\paragraph{Realism versus usefulness.} The generator ranking for a fixed tree classifier differs from a ranking by
realism. Diffusion is the only generator that follows a requested severity and produces nearly
indistinguishable, physically valid rows; it is therefore the right tool when synthetic weather must be
plausible (for example for scenario analysis). For the narrow purpose of improving LightGBM, cheaper generators
with EVT tail selection were at least as good.

\paragraph{Earlier negative results for CTGAN.} In Part I CTGAN data hurt the classifier. That comparison used
8.7 synthetic rows per real extreme and one seed; under the fixed protocol of Part II (one synthetic per real
extreme, the same physics clean-up for all generators, five seeds), CTGAN helps. The amount of synthetic data
and the evaluation protocol matter as much as the generator.

\paragraph{How much synthetic data.} Synthetic extremes are useful in moderation. When they outnumber real
extremes 8.7 to 1, onset skill falls in every seed and the EVT advantage disappears.

\paragraph{Persistence.} Next-day heat at a grid cell is dominated by persistence: the strongest single predictor
is how far today already is above tomorrow's threshold. The models' advantage over persistence is therefore
modest in absolute terms (PR-AUC +0.02--0.03, similar CSI) and lies mainly in heatwave onsets, which persistence
cannot anticipate, and, with EVT augmentation, in no longer falling behind it on the most severe days.

\paragraph{Lessons on evaluation.} Month-block bootstrap intervals are wide because the test decade contains a
limited number of independent heat events, and a one-seed result that looked convincing did not hold across
seeds. Effects on rare extremes should be judged over several seeds or with seed-averaged models, with all
choices made on a separate validation period.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Labels and features come from a reanalysis; a check of severe heat days against station observations
        (Shimla, Sundernagar, Bhuntar) has not been run.
  \item The test decade has twice the training rate of heat extremes; no model was adapted to this shift.
  \item Only next-day seasonal heat extremes were modelled.
  \item EVT guides generation at sampling or selection time; it is not part of any generator's training loss.
  \item For unconditional generators, EVT tail selection relies on a severity judge trained on real extremes;
        the judge rates synthetic rows as more severe than real ones, so their tail positions are approximate.
  \item The tail-focus setting (half from the upper 20\%) was fixed by hand; only the amount of synthetic data
        was chosen on validation.
  \item The explanation of why copies beat weights (LightGBM's minimum leaf size counts rows) was not tested.
  \item One classifier family (LightGBM) and one case study; an MLP did not benefit from synthetic data.
  \item Free Colab resources (T4 GPU or two CPU cores) limited training data (half of 1981--2010), generator
        size and the number of seeds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and future work}
Treating the minority class as a tail and using an extreme value model to decide which extremes to add
improves the prediction of the most severe next-day heat extremes in Himachal Pradesh, robustly across
training seeds. With a fixed LightGBM classifier, EVT tail focus improved every one of six generators, most
consistently on heatwave onsets, and CTGAN and TVAE with EVT tail focus gave the best results (severe PR-AUC
0.601 against 0.441 for real data only), beating a strong persistence baseline on overall and onset skill. The
most realistic generator (diffusion) was not the most useful for the classifier. A control without any generator
showed that the EVT tail emphasis is itself the main active ingredient: copies of real extremes chosen with the EVT
tail target beat random copies on severe days in every seed and gave the best overall PR-AUC of the study, while
generators with EVT tail focus added a further gain on the most severe days at the cost of heatwave-onset skill.

Future work: station validation of the severe events; choosing the tail emphasis on validation; a GPD term
inside generator training; spatial models that use neighbouring cells; longer lead times, where persistence is
weaker; and public imbalanced benchmarks to test the framework beyond weather data.
