\begin{abstract}
The minority class in an imbalanced classification problem is often the extreme tail of the data, yet
standard remedies (class weights, SMOTE) and generic deep generative models do not model tail behaviour.
We treat the minority class as a tail phenomenon, describe its severity with a Generalized Pareto
Distribution (GPD) fitted by peaks-over-threshold, and use this description to decide which synthetic
minority examples a deep generative model produces. The case study is next-day extreme heat on a
\ang{0.1} ERA5-Land grid over Himachal Pradesh (529 cells, 1981--2025, about 1\% positives), with
season-wise extremes defined from 1981--2010 thresholds and a test decade (2016--2025) that contains
twice as many extremes as the training period.
After two GAN-based attempts (CTGAN with EVT resampling, and severity-conditioned CTGAN) failed to produce
controllable or physically valid extremes, we built a conditional tabular diffusion model whose
condition is the season and the standardised severity, so that EVT chooses exactly which severities are
generated; physical consistency is enforced by construction.
With LightGBM and five training seeds, adding one synthetic extreme per real one, with severities drawn
from the fitted GPD with emphasis on its upper tail, raises PR-AUC on the most severe 10\% of test
extremes from 0.411 to 0.549 (better in 5 of 5 seeds), and by 0.066 over the same diffusion model fed
the empirical severities (5 of 5 seeds), without loss of overall or onset skill. The seed-averaged model
reaches PR-AUC 0.609 against 0.586 for persistence (better in 97\% of month-block resamples) and onset
PR-AUC 0.228 against 0.191 (100\%), while matching persistence on severe days. Sampling the GPD without
tail emphasis, SMOTE, class weights and CTGAN give no reliable gain; large synthetic shares harm onset
prediction; and a single training seed was shown to be insufficient to judge tail effects.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In imbalanced classification the class of interest is rare and therefore poorly learned. Common
remedies re-weight the loss or create synthetic minority samples by interpolation
(SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote}). Deep generative models such as GANs~\cite{goodfellow2014gan,xu2019ctgan}
and diffusion models~\cite{ho2020ddpm,kotelnikov2023tabddpm} can produce richer synthetic data, but they
are trained to match the bulk of the distribution and tend to under-represent its tails, which is where
the minority class often lives. Extreme value theory (EVT)~\cite{coles2001} offers an asymptotically
justified model for exactly that region.

This project builds a framework that couples EVT with deep generative models so that synthetic minority
data are statistically consistent with real extremes, and tests whether this improves a classifier:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item treat the minority class as a tail phenomenon;
  \item use EVT to characterise and constrain that tail;
  \item use generative models to augment and rebalance the training data;
  \item verify that the synthetic data reproduce the extreme behaviour, and that they help.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A case study with a demanding, realistic set-up: per-cell, per-season heat extremes on a
        45-year \ang{0.1} grid, a strict temporal split and a non-stationary test decade.
  \item A standardisation of excesses by each cell's own variability that turns spuriously heavy pooled
        tails into the bounded tails expected for temperature (Section~\ref{sec:evt}).
  \item An EVT-guided conditional tabular diffusion model in which the GPD decides which severities are
        generated, with physics enforced by construction and a direct check that generated rows match
        the requested severity (Section~\ref{sec:diff}).
  \item A careful evaluation: onset and severe-extreme scores, month-block bootstrap intervals, paired
        tests, a same-generator ablation (EVT vs no EVT), a ratio sweep and a five-seed check, with all
        choices made on a validation period (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Related work.}
Elor and Averbuch-Elor~\cite{elor2022smote} found that balancing rarely helps strong classifiers such as
gradient-boosted trees, which matches our baselines. D'souza et al.~\cite{dsouza2025overlap} report that
tabular diffusion produces better minority samples than GANs. On the EVT side, ExGAN~\cite{bhatia2021exgan}
generates extreme samples of a chosen severity, evtGAN~\cite{boulaguiem2022evtgan} combines GANs with
extreme-value margins and dependence for spatial weather extremes, and heavy-tailed diffusion
models~\cite{pandey2025heavy} change the noise distribution to reach far tails. Jalalzai et
al.~\cite{jalalzai2018extreme} study classification in extreme regions of the input space. These works
generate or classify extremes; few test whether EVT-consistent synthetic extremes improve a downstream
classifier, which is the question addressed here. For extreme-heat forecasting, neural weather models
are known to under-predict the most extreme events~\cite{lopezgomez2023heat}.

\subsection{Pre-processing and verification}
All inputs are on ERA5-Land's \ang{0.1} grid over \ang{30.3}--\ang{33.3}N, \ang{75.5}--\ang{79.1}E;
accumulated variables (rain, radiation) are shifted back by the one day by which ERA5-Land stamps them
late; SRTM is averaged into cell elevation and slope; cells outside the state are masked, leaving 529
cells and 16{,}436 days. The processed file was verified against the raw ERA5-Land files for five cells
(522--\SI{5279}{m}) and six years: all variables match after unit conversion, with no day where
$T_{\min}>T_{\max}$. Mean temperature and humidity were missing for 1981--1985 and were refilled from
the raw files. A bias correction against MODIS from an earlier pipeline was not used: its validation
against IMD showed a $-15$\,\textdegree C bias that is most plausibly the IMD missing-value code (99.9)
treated as data.

\section{Problem formulation}
\subsection{Extreme labels}
For cell $c$ and day $t$ let $T^{\max}_{c,t}$ be the daily maximum temperature. A threshold
$u_{c,g}$ is the 99th percentile of $T^{\max}$ over the days of group $g$ in the base period 1981--2010,
and day $t$ is a heat extreme if $T^{\max}_{c,t}>u_{c,g(t)}$. The main grouping is the IMD season:
winter (Dec--Feb), pre-monsoon (Mar--May), monsoon (Jun--Sep), post-monsoon (Oct--Nov); each season is
compared only with the same season. Whole-year and day-of-year ($\pm7$ days) labels, and cold extremes
(1st percentile of $T^{\min}$), were also built. Using only 1981--2010 for thresholds keeps the
validation and test years unseen. Thresholds fall by about 6--7\,\textdegree C per km of altitude
(Table~\ref{tab:thr}), consistent with the atmospheric lapse rate.

\subsection{Prediction task, features and splits}
Each sample is one cell on day $t$; the target is whether day $t+1$ is a seasonal heat extreme. The final
feature set (dataset v3) has 57 features:
\begin{itemize}
  \item terrain: latitude, longitude, elevation, slope;
  \item weather on days $t$, $t-1$, $t-2$: Tmax, Tmin, Tmean, dewpoint, humidity, pressure, solar
        radiation, soil moisture, rain; today's Tmax and Tmin anomalies;
  \item longer history: mean Tmax anomaly over 7, 14 and 30 days, Tmin anomaly over 7 and 30 days,
        length of the current warm spell, days since the last extreme, extremes in the last 30 days, rain
        over 7 and 30 days, soil moisture over 14 and 30 days and its 14-day change, 7-day dewpoint and
        radiation, 3-day pressure change;
  \item distance of today, yesterday and the day before to tomorrow's threshold, in \textdegree C and in
        units of the cell's variability $s_{c,g}$, plus their trend and the day-to-day Tmax change.
\end{itemize}
Calendar features are excluded: in a first version the model relied on day of year (39\% of split gain),
which does not transfer to a warmer test decade. The data are split by year, because neighbouring days
and cells are strongly correlated (one heatwave makes hundreds of cells extreme at once). Training uses a
random half of the 1981--2010 cell-days to fit free compute.

\subsection{What the labels show}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Warming.} Heat extremes nearly double in the test decade (1.94\% vs 1.02\%) while cold
        extremes fall. 2024 has the most widespread heat.
  \item \textbf{Strongest signal in October--November.} Post-monsoon heat extremes reach 11.0\% of
        days in 2020 and 6--7\% in 2017, 2021 and 2024, against a 1\% baseline.
  \item \textbf{Clustering.} On 6 October 1983, 522 of 529 cells were extreme together; the training
        positives come from a few hundred events.
  \item \textbf{Season-edge effect.} Seasonal extremes gather at the warm edge of a season (pre-monsoon
        heat in May, post-monsoon heat in early October).
\end{itemize}

\section{Extreme value model of the minority class}\label{sec:evt}
\subsection{Peaks over threshold}
By the Pickands--Balkema--de Haan theorem~\cite{pickands1975,balkema1974}, for a high threshold $u$
the excess $Y=X-u$ given $X>u$ is approximately Generalized Pareto distributed:
\begin{equation}
  F_{\xi,\sigma}(y)=1-\left(1+\frac{\xi y}{\sigma}\right)^{-1/\xi},\qquad y>0,\; 1+\xi y/\sigma>0 .
\end{equation}
The shape $\xi$ determines the tail: $\xi<0$ gives a bounded tail with upper end point $\sigma/|\xi|$
(expected for temperature), $\xi=0$ an exponential tail and $\xi>0$ a heavy tail. Parameters are fitted
by maximum likelihood on 1981--2010 exceedances, with confidence intervals from a block bootstrap that
resamples whole days (exceedances are spatially clustered) and threshold-stability checks.

\subsection{Standardised severity}
Pooling raw excesses from all 529 cells mixes low cells with small day-to-day variability and high
cells with large variability; the mixture looks heavy-tailed even when each cell's tail is bounded. We
therefore standardise each excess by the cell's own variability $s_{c,g}$ (the standard deviation of its
daily temperature anomaly in that season, 1981--2010):
\begin{equation}
  z_{c,t}=\frac{T^{\max}_{c,t}-u_{c,g(t)}}{s_{c,g(t)}} .
\end{equation}
This severity $z$ is the quantity the generative models are conditioned on and the EVT model describes.
It is never a classifier feature, since it encodes the next day's outcome.

\section{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
All classifiers are trained on 1981--2010. Every choice (early stopping, decision threshold, amount of
synthetic data, blend weight, final model) is made on the validation years 2011--2015; the test decade
2016--2025 is used only for scoring. Scores:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PR-AUC} (average precision) on all test days; a random classifier scores 0.019.
  \item \textbf{Onset PR-AUC}: only days where today is not yet above tomorrow's threshold, so that
        persistence cannot simply carry an ongoing event forward.
  \item \textbf{Severe PR-AUC}: normal days against the 10\% most severe test extremes
        ($z\ge0.77$, 3{,}745 cell-days); this is the extreme region the EVT guidance targets.
  \item Precision, recall and the critical success index
        $\mathrm{CSI}=\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN})$ at the F1-optimal validation threshold.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Uncertainty.} Because extremes come in spatially and temporally clustered events, 95\% intervals
resample whole test months (200 resamples). Two models are compared on the same resamples; the share of
resamples in which one is better is reported, with $\ge0.95$ read as a convincing win and $\le0.05$ as a
convincing loss. From Step~9 on we also repeat training with several seeds, since the bootstrap does not
capture training randomness.

\textbf{Baseline to beat.} \emph{Persistence} uses no model: its score is today's distance to tomorrow's
threshold, $T^{\max}_{c,t}-u_{c,g(t+1)}$. Because heat extremes persist, this simple rule is strong.

\section{Classic baselines and GAN-based generators}
\subsection{Classic rebalancing}
With the distance-to-threshold features, LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} (learning rate 0.05, up to
1{,}000 trees with early stopping, and a monotone constraint that a larger distance can only raise the
risk) gains 0.08--0.11 PR-AUC over a first version and the standardised distance carries 78\% of the
split gain. Class weights and SMOTE give no reliable improvement (Table~\ref{tab:step8}), in line
with~\cite{elor2022smote}.

\subsection{CTGAN with EVT resampling (Step 4)}
A CTGAN~\cite{xu2019ctgan} was trained on the real extremes (features plus season and $z$). Three
synthetic sets came from the same generator: as generated; with physically impossible rows removed; and,
the EVT-guided variant, resampled per season so that $z$ follows the fitted GPD (20 bins of equal GPD
probability, each sample weighted by target share over generated share, nothing beyond the tail bound).
Only 15.9\% of the raw CTGAN rows were physically possible (for example $T^{\min}>T^{\max}$). EVT
resampling made the synthetic severities follow the GPD (Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance from 0.16--0.24
down to about 0.02), but the GAN never generated the far tail, so EVT could only re-weight what was
there. All variants were within 0.015 PR-AUC of each other and of real data alone.

\subsection{Severity-conditioned CTGAN (Step 5)}
Conditioning CTGAN on season $\times$ GPD severity bin, with physics enforced by snapping rows to real
cells, also failed: 70\% of generated rows had a severity outside the requested bin, because CTGAN
guarantees only its discrete conditioning column. With month-block intervals of about $\pm0.07$, no
method was statistically better than real data alone or than persistence. This motivated a generator in
which the severity is a direct, continuous input.

\section{EVT-guided conditional tabular diffusion}\label{sec:diff}
\subsection{Model}
The generator is a denoising diffusion model~\cite{ho2020ddpm} trained on the 29{,}604 real training
extremes. It models the 49 base features (terrain, lagged weather, long-history features) after a
quantile transform to a standard normal per feature. The forward process adds Gaussian noise,
$q(x_\tau\mid x_0)=\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_\tau}\,x_0,(1-\bar\alpha_\tau)I)$, with a cosine schedule
over $T=1000$ steps~\cite{nichol2021improved}. The denoiser $\epsilon_\theta(x_\tau,\tau,c)$ is a residual
MLP (width 512, four blocks) with sinusoidal step embedding and the condition
\begin{equation}
  c=\big[\,\text{one-hot season},\; z/0.5,\; 1\,\big],
\end{equation}
which is set to zero for 10\% of training rows so that the same network also learns the unconditional
model. It is trained with the standard noise-prediction loss
$\lVert\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_\tau,\tau,c)\rVert^2$ (15{,}000 steps, batch 1{,}024, AdamW, EMA of
the weights, about 30 minutes on a free T4 GPU). Samples are drawn with 50 DDIM steps~\cite{song2021ddim}
and classifier-free guidance~\cite{ho2022cfg} of weight $w=2$:
$\hat\epsilon=(1+w)\,\epsilon_\theta(x,\tau,c)-w\,\epsilon_\theta(x,\tau,\varnothing)$.

\subsection{Physics by construction}
Each generated row is snapped to the nearest real grid cell (which fixes its terrain, threshold and
variability), the three temperatures of each day are sorted so that $T^{\min}\le T^{\text{mean}}\le
T^{\max}$, other variables are clipped to their physical ranges, and the eight distance-to-threshold
features are computed exactly from the generated temperatures. Only 0.0--0.2\% of diffusion rows needed
their temperatures reordered, against 13--31\% of CTGAN rows.

\subsection{Where EVT enters: which severities are requested}
Because $z$ is an input, the training set's severity distribution can be chosen freely. Three synthetic
sets come from the same trained network, so any difference is due to the EVT guidance alone:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{ddpm\_emp} (no EVT): season and $z$ copied from randomly chosen real extremes;
  \item \texttt{ddpm\_evt}: season by its real share, $z$ drawn from the fitted seasonal GPD;
  \item \texttt{ddpm\_evt\_tail} (EVT with tail emphasis): half of the requests from the GPD, half from
        its upper 20\% (GPD probability in $[0.8,1)$). About 60\% of these synthetic extremes therefore lie
        in the region that holds 20\% of real extremes.
\end{itemize}
GPD draws are capped at the bounded tail's end point and at 1.5 times the largest observed severity of
the season, so EVT can extrapolate moderately beyond the record (0.2--0.6\% of rows in winter and
post-monsoon lie beyond the observed maximum). Each set holds 256{,}428 rows (8.7 per real extreme), of
which a random subset is used at lower ratios.

\subsection{Does the model obey the requested severity?}
A separate LightGBM ``judge'' trained on real extremes predicts $z$ from the features of held-out real
extremes and of generated rows; the Spearman correlation between requested and judged severity measures
control. On held-out real extremes it is 0.776; for the diffusion sets 0.632 (\texttt{emp}), 0.616
(\texttt{evt}) and 0.666 (\texttt{evt\_tail}); for CTGAN 0.043. The diffusion model therefore produces
rows whose weather actually corresponds to the requested severity, which CTGAN does not. The severities
requested by \texttt{ddpm\_evt} follow the fitted GPD to a Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance of about 0.003.

Three observations shaped the next steps. (i) Plain LightGBM is much weaker than persistence on the most
severe extremes (0.461 vs 0.565): with few deep-tail examples, the trees do not extrapolate. EVT diffusion
closed this gap (0.580; a paired gain of +0.11 over no-EVT diffusion in 100\% of month resamples).
(ii) With 8.7 synthetic rows per real extreme, onset skill fell (0.227 to 0.182). (iii) Every synthetic set
hurt the MLP, which, unlike the monotone-constrained trees, can learn to separate synthetic from real rows
when they dominate the minority class. CTGAN was the worst method for both classifiers. The rest of the
study therefore uses LightGBM.

\subsection{How much synthetic data (Step 9)}
Figure~\ref{fig:ratio} varies the number of synthetic extremes per real one. Small amounts (0.25, 0.5)
change little. At one synthetic per real extreme, only the tail-emphasised EVT set lifts severe PR-AUC
(0.560, against 0.441 and 0.449 for the no-EVT and plain-EVT sets) while keeping onset skill; at the full
amount every set loses onset skill. On validation, the tail-emphasised set at ratio 1 was the best
single model.

being measured. Every setting was therefore trained with five seeds; the seed changes both LightGBM's

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{Why tail emphasis, not plain EVT sampling.} The fitted GPD describes the observed severities
well (that is what a good fit means), so drawing from it reproduces almost the same distribution as
copying real severities, and adds little information. The useful role of EVT here is to tell the generator
\emph{where} the real data are sparse and how the tail continues: requesting 60\% of synthetic extremes from
the upper 20\% of the GPD fills exactly the region where LightGBM has too few examples to learn, and the
bounded GPD with a cap keeps the extrapolation physically plausible. A diffusion model is necessary for
this: it follows the requested severity (Spearman 0.62--0.67), whereas CTGAN ignored it (0.04).

\paragraph{How much synthetic data.} Synthetic extremes are useful in moderation. When they outnumber real
extremes 8.7 to 1, the classifier increasingly learns the synthetic distribution: onset skill falls by
0.03 in every seed and the EVT advantage disappears. One synthetic per real extreme was best for both
severe and onset skill.

\paragraph{Persistence.} Next-day heat at a grid cell is dominated by persistence: the strongest single
predictor is how far today already is above tomorrow's threshold. The models' advantage over persistence
is therefore modest in absolute terms (PR-AUC +0.02, CSI 0.405 for both at their operating points) and
lies mainly in onset days, which persistence cannot anticipate, and, with EVT augmentation, in no longer
falling behind it on the most severe days.

\paragraph{Lessons on evaluation.} Month-block bootstrap intervals are wide ($\pm0.07$--0.2) because the
test decade contains a limited number of independent heat events. A paired test on one training run
measured test-sample uncertainty but not training randomness, and a one-seed result that looked
convincing (share 1.00) did not hold up across seeds. Effects on rare extremes should be judged over
several seeds or with seed-averaged models.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Labels and features come from a reanalysis; a check of the severe heat days against station
        observations (Shimla, Sundernagar, Bhuntar) has not been run yet.
  \item The test decade has twice the training rate of heat extremes. All models are trained on
        1981--2010 and face this shift; no model was adapted to it.
  \item Only next-day seasonal heat extremes were modelled; cold tails are not yet captured by a single
        GPD.
  \item EVT guides the generator at sampling time; it is not part of the diffusion training loss.
  \item The tail-emphasis setting (half of the requests from the upper 20\%) and the ratio grid were
        fixed by hand; only the ratio was chosen on validation.
  \item The MLP did not benefit from synthetic data in any form; whether a different neural classifier,
        loss weighting of synthetic rows, or the overlap-class trick of~\cite{dsouza2025overlap} changes
        this is open.
  \item The general-purpose claim of the framework is supported by one case study; public imbalanced
        benchmarks were not tested.
  \item All experiments run on free Colab resources (T4 GPU or two CPU cores), which limited model size,
        training data (half of 1981--2010) and the number of seeds.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and future work}
Treating the minority class as a tail and using EVT to decide which severities a conditional diffusion
model generates improves the prediction of the most severe next-day heat extremes in Himachal Pradesh,
robustly across training seeds, provided that EVT is used to emphasise the sparse upper tail and that
synthetic data do not outnumber real extremes. The resulting seed-averaged model is the only one in this
study that beats a strong persistence baseline on overall and onset skill without losing to it on the
most severe days. Classic rebalancing, GAN-based generation and plain GPD sampling gave no reliable gain.

Future work: station validation of the severe events; a GPD-likelihood term inside diffusion training;
choosing the tail emphasis on validation; cold extremes and longer lead times; and public imbalanced
benchmarks to test the framework beyond weather data.
